01 · Overview

The flow changed. The screens didn't.

Project overview

A new registration flow changed which screens each account type sees, but the interface hadn't caught up. It was also stiff and text-heavy, with almost no graphics to help people compare plans.

I redesigned the path from landing on the site to paying, so students, parents and teachers can pick the right account, register with less effort, and see what a paid plan adds before they pay.

My role

This was an assignment, not a problem I picked, so I started by framing it: what I owned, who I could ask, and where my responsibility ended. I worked through personas, journey maps, a conceptual model, user flows and a heuristic redesign, and took every round to the UI/UX team lead.

02 · Research

Players and payers are different people.

Players are students aged 10–16. Payers are parents, teachers and schools. I wrote a persona for each, covering what they want from an educational game, what they want to avoid, and what they need but weren't getting.

03 · Model & flows

Fewer objects to learn, one flow per account.

Conceptual model

I mapped everything a user can do into an object–action matrix. Consolidating overlaps gave users less to learn and the team one vocabulary: six objects (organization, district, school, teachers, students, analytics) and a small set of actions (manage, export, approve or reject).

Reframe

For district admins, managing schools and monitoring adoption are frequent, while payment and renewal are rare. So procurement didn't belong in self-serve sign-up. Admin accounts now go to a sales-assisted "Contact us" path, leaving three self-serve flows: Student, Personal and Teacher.

04 · Design system

Each color has one job.

Colors were one-off hex values. I captured them as tokens with a clear action hierarchy, and applied them first to sign-in, the screen every account sees.

Every change grounded in a usability principle.

Role selection: fewer, better-ordered choices

BeforeAfterWhy it works
Admin sat beside the self-serve roles, though it needs custom pricing.Admin moves to a "Contact us" path.Hick's Law. A smaller choice set shortens decision time; Admin stays reachable.
Roles weren't ordered by use.Student first, Teacher last.Serial position. People remember first and last items.
A mis-tap went straight into the wrong flow.A "Confirm" step to review the choice.Error prevention. A mistake costs one click, not two pages.
No route to plan details.A "Pricing page" link below the CTA.Information scent at the moment of decision.

Teacher sign-up: 17 fields on two pages became 12 on one

The original split credentials (page 1) from a "Tell us more about yourself" questionnaire (page 2). Below, page 2 sits beside the new single page.

BeforeAfterWhy it works
Credentials, then a separate questionnaire.One page with the full form.Visibility of status. On a PC-first product, users see the whole task and a mid-flow exit disappears.
17 fields, incl. grades taught, student count, demographics.12 fields, about 30% fewer.Interaction cost. Expedia credited removing one optional field with $12M a year.
Identical full-width fields.Widths match input; short pairs share a row.Proximity. Width signals how much to type, in line with Baymard's form research.
Password typed twice.Confirm-email instead.Error prevention. A wrong email locks users out; a wrong password is easy to reset.
No Terms or Privacy notice.Notice under the CTA, plus "Log in here."Transparency. Meets a requirement and gives a way out.

Upgrade: show the value before asking for payment

BeforeAfterWhy it works

08 · Lesson learned

A tight scope is a design constraint.

I couldn't change prices or tiers, so every improvement had to come from clarity: what people see, in what order, and how few decisions they make.

01

Protect the foundation

Two months was tight, but I kept the empathize phase. It kept the design on real needs and gave the wider team shared context.

02

Communicate proactively

My team lead taught me UX skills and how to work with others in a way that makes everyone more productive.

03

Iteration isn't failure

In school, another round means the work wasn't good enough. In a product it's expected. V2 came from reviewing V1 critically.

Next time

Run usability tests with students and teachers on the new flows, and set up sign-up and upgrade analytics before launch so the impact can be measured.
